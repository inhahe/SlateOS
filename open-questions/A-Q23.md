## A-Q23 — [A] Two unused "who may do what" systems would become a second and third set of rules. Make them live, merge one into what already works, or remove them? — Status: OPEN

**In short:** your A-Q21 answer (design-decisions §978) asked me to fix and
switch on several security modules that nothing used. Four of them are now
live. Two are not like the others: `capsettings` and `secpolicy` are each a
whole *rule system* of their own, and each overlaps rules the kernel already
enforces. Switching them on as written would give the system three separate
sets of permission rules, each able to refuse what another allows. I think the
right result is different from "switch them on", so I am asking rather than
doing it.

**Terms, glossed:**
- *capability* (here): a token a program holds that lets it use one thing --
  a file, a network port. The kernel's whole permission model is built on
  these, and they are enforced today.
- *capability group*: a named set of users allowed into tagged files and
  folders (`cap::groups`, `cap::file_tags`) -- enforced today, on every path.
- *MAC* (mandatory access control): rules written by the administrator that
  even a file's owner cannot loosen, keyed by *labels* stuck on programs and
  files. SELinux is the best-known one.

**What each is.**

| module | what it holds | overlaps |
|---|---|---|
| `capsettings` | its own list of 20 permissions (network, reboot, change the clock ...), its own groups, per-user and per-program grants, and per-path requirements with `*` wildcards | its path requirements are what `cap::file_tags` already enforces; its groups duplicate `cap::groups`; its per-program grants are what a process's capabilities already are |
| `secpolicy` | labels on programs and files, allow/deny/audit rules, and a Disabled / Permissive / Enforcing switch | a full MAC framework: every permission check in the kernel would ask it too |

| option | *What changes:* | for | against |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Merge `capsettings` into the enforced tables; park `secpolicy`** (recommended) | the settings panel shows and edits the groups and tags the kernel already enforces; `secpolicy` stays unconnected, marked as a future MAC, and leaves A-Q21's list | one set of rules, which a user can understand and the kernel enforces; nothing duplicated | `capsettings`'s per-user "Network" / "Reboot" style permissions have no enforced counterpart yet, and would wait for one |
| B. Connect both as written | a program can be refused by any of three systems | what §978's text says | three sources of truth for "may I"; the two new ones are keyed by text patterns, which is how a rename slips past a rule |
| C. Remove both | the panels lose their settings | nothing pretends to protect | loses `secpolicy`'s design, which is a reasonable start on a MAC if you want one later |

**My recommendation: A.** It is what I did for the same problem with file
immutability (§1525): where two models record one concept, keep the one the
kernel enforces and make the other a view of it. A MAC is a large, separate
decision -- what labels, who writes the policy, what the default is -- and
`deferred-questions.md` is the honest place for it until someone wants one.

**Where it bites:** `kernel/src/fs/capsettings.rs`, `kernel/src/fs/secpolicy.rs`;
the enforced tables are `kernel/src/cap/groups.rs` and
`kernel/src/cap/file_tags.rs`.

**If this is never answered:** nothing breaks. Both modules stay as they are --
recorded and unenforced, and saying so in their own documentation. Nothing
else is waiting on this: I am continuing with A-Q21's other items (the
`acl`, `fcomment`, `queryable` and `tags` doors) meanwhile.
