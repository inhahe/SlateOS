## A-Q30 — [A] Should a device be able to reach all of its Linux driver's memory, or only the buffers it is handed? — Status: OPEN (raised 2026-10-29)

**In short:** a network card, Wi-Fi chip or GPU reads and writes memory by
itself ("DMA"), and SlateOS lets a device reach only the memory its driver
was given for it. For speed, the layer that runs Linux's drivers (decision
1586) gives the device *all* of its driver program's memory -- the packets,
but also the driver's own records and the addresses of its code. Nothing is
wrong with a working device; a faulty or hostile one could overwrite those
records and take over its driver program. You are asked whether that is
acceptable, or whether the device should reach only the buffers it is
handed, at the price of copying data.

**Terms.** *DMA* (direct memory access): a device reading or writing memory
itself, without the processor. *IOMMU*: the hardware that limits which
memory each device can reach. *Bounce buffer*: memory the device can reach,
into which data is copied on its way to the device and out of which it is
copied on its way back. *Built-in* versus *external* device: soldered or
slotted inside the PC, versus plugged in from outside (Thunderbolt, USB4).

**Where things stand.** Every device is held to its own memory by the IOMMU
(decision 1576), so a device can never reach the kernel or another program.
What is in question is only the driver program the device belongs to: under
the current design the device can reach all of it. The one driver running
today (a test driver) is built in; the first real one, the e1000e network
driver, is next.

| Option | *What changes:* |
|---|---|
| A. As now: the device reaches its driver's whole memory | Packets go to and from the card with no copying. A broken or hostile card can take over its driver program, and then do what that program may: talk to the network daemon as a network card -- little more than a hostile card can do with the packets it sends anyway. |
| B. The device reaches only what it is handed, always | The driver's own records are out of every device's reach. Each packet is copied once on its way through: about 2% of one core at 1 Gb/s, about 20% at 10 Gb/s, and more for a GPU. |
| C. Packet memory reachable, the driver's records not | Most packets go uncopied, and the driver's records and code addresses are out of the device's reach. The line between the two is drawn by how Linux allocates memory, so some memory that is not a packet stays reachable; more code in the layer than A or B. |
| **D. B for external devices, A for built-in ones** (what Linux does) | Plugged-in devices -- the realistic DMA attack: a malicious Thunderbolt gadget -- reach only what they are handed, and pay the copy; built-in cards run uncopied, trusted as much as the PC's own hardware is. |

- **A** is the fastest and the simplest, and is what runs now. Its risk is
  real only for a device that is broken or built to attack.
- **B** is the safest, and what Linux does in confidential virtual machines,
  at a cost a 10 Gb/s card or a GPU would feel.
- **C** keeps most of A's speed and most of B's safety, at the price of a
  boundary that is a heuristic rather than a rule.
- **D** spends the copy only where an attacker can plug something in. It
  needs the kernel to know which PCI devices are external, which the
  firmware says (an ACPI property on the port) and the kernel does not read
  yet.
- My recommendation: **D**. Built-in devices stay as fast as they are now,
  and the copy is added when the first driver for a pluggable device comes;
  until then, D is A.

**If it is never answered:** nothing breaks and nothing is blocked; the
design stays A. The risk exists only for a device that is broken or hostile,
and no driver for a pluggable device exists yet.

**Where it bites:** `linuxdrv/emul/lx_dma.c` (Linux's DMA calls),
`linuxdrv/kit/src/sys.rs` (where `kmalloc`'s memory comes from),
`linuxdrv/emul/lx_pci.c`; `kernel/src/pci.rs` for which devices are external.
Decision 1586 records the current design.
