## F-Q14 — A machine to test SlateOS on real hardware: this PC, once your new one arrives, with a KVM box? — OPEN

**In short:** SlateOS has only ever run inside an emulator, and an emulator
cannot stand in for real graphics chips, so drivers for them cannot be
written or tested today. To test on real hardware without you at the
keyboard, Claude needs a second computer it can restart and watch while it
keeps working on the first. You are getting a new computer soon and moving
your graphics card to it. The suggestion: the project moves to the new
computer, and this PC becomes the test machine, with a **KVM box** attached (a
small network device that captures a computer's screen and acts as its
keyboard, mouse and USB stick, so another computer can operate it). Asked in
chat on 2026-10-10, where the options below came out of your questions.

**Options.**

- **1. This PC becomes the test machine, with a KVM box; development moves
  to the new computer** (recommended).
  *What changes:* Claude boots SlateOS on real hardware on its own, as often
  as needed -- sees the real screen, types, restarts the machine when
  SlateOS hangs -- with your new computer never at risk. This PC tests its
  own Intel graphics first (switched on in the firmware once the RTX 4090
  moves out), and its free graphics-card slot takes whatever card is tested
  next: an AMD card, or a cheap RTX 20-series or later for NVIDIA. A spare
  SSD in it takes installation tests without touching the Windows disk.
  Costs the KVM box -- roughly $70 to $400 by model at 2025 prices; PiKVM
  or JetKVM, not NanoKVM, which had security problems reported in 2025 --
  plus its power-control add-on or a smart plug. The project's move to the
  new computer (toolchains, the six sessions, the same `E:\visual studio
  projects` path so no script breaks) is Claude's to do.
- **2. A separate small test machine with a KVM box.** A used office mini-PC
  with recent Intel graphics.
  *What changes:* the same as 1 for Intel graphics, but a mini-PC has no
  slot for a graphics card, so AMD and NVIDIA cards cannot be tested on it;
  about $150 to $350 more than 1.
- **3. No KVM box: you boot SlateOS on this PC from a USB stick when you are
  at it** (design-decisions §263's plan, as written).
  *What changes:* nothing to buy; every real-hardware test waits for you,
  and Windows -- and with it the six sessions -- is off while SlateOS runs.

Ruled out in the same conversation: a virtual machine given the real
graphics chip. Windows (WSL included) cannot give a virtual machine a
physical graphics device; Windows Server's Hyper-V can for some devices but
refuses Intel's integrated graphics, and SlateOS has no drivers for Hyper-V's
virtual hardware; Linux on this PC could, at the cost of this PC's operating
system.

**What a test machine is for, in order.** Graphics drivers follow what
people have, not what this PC has: Intel integrated graphics first (most PCs,
laptops above all), then AMD, then NVIDIA. NVIDIA's is the hardest: the
realistic route is the open-source stack Linux now has for RTX 20-series and
later (the nouveau driver running NVIDIA's own GPU firmware, and Mesa's NVK).
Until a card has a driver, SlateOS still shows its desktop on it, through the
display the firmware sets up at power-on -- at a fixed resolution, with no
acceleration -- as its boot console does today.

**Recommendation:** 1.

**If this is never answered:** nothing breaks and nothing gets worse, but
everything stays inside the emulator: no driver for a real graphics chip can
be written, and the first real-hardware boot keeps waiting for you at the
machine (ready since 2026-08-21, `bare-metal-boot.md`).

**Where it bites:** design-decisions §263 (this PC's bare metal, the Intel
driver written against the real chip -- option 1 keeps both and automates
them) and §268 (the bootable USB image); `bare-metal-boot.md`; the roadmap's
GPU driver items (lane A's Intel, AMD and NVIDIA lines) and the Mesa port
(lane F).
